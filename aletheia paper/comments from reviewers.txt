The paper addresses a highly critical and practical problem: the deployment of medical Large Language Models (LLMs) in resource-constrained African clinical settings. A major strength of this study is its strict adherence to realistic hardware constraints; it successfully fine-tunes and quantizes a 3B parameter model (Aletheia, based on Qwen2.5-3B) that operates completely offline on a standard 8GB RAM laptop, consuming less than 4GB of memory. Furthermore, the methodology demonstrates clear scientific rigor by conducting an ablation-style comparison against its zero-shot base model, unequivocally proving that the fine-tuning process yielded substantial gains in clinical reasoning and Top-1 accuracy.
•       Text vs. Figure Contradiction: There is a glaring contradiction regarding reasoning task performance. The text claims the model "performs most strongly on test recommendation and evidence update tasks". However, the corresponding chart (Fig 5 — Accuracy by Reasoning Task Type) explicitly shows that "test recommendation" has the lowest Top-1 accuracy of all tasks, at just 0.200 (20%).
•       Figure Numbering: The text references "Fig. 7" for the reasoning task performance, but the embedded chart for this data is labeled "Fig 5".
•       Inflated Accuracy based on Synthetic Data: The authors report an impressive 100% Top-3 diagnostic accuracy and 80% Top-1 accuracy, and state this represents a "genuine decision-support capability". However, this is an over-claim at this stage, as the authors acknowledge in their limitations that the data is synthetically generated from handcrafted templates reflecting their own clinical knowledge, rather than empirical patient cohorts.
•       Clinical Deployability: While the hardware deployment is proven, claiming the system reduces the "cognitive load of generating a differential from scratch" is premature, given that formal multi-site clinical validation with a defined patient cohort has not yet been completed.
•       Lack of Strong Baselines: The study only compares the fine-tuned Aletheia model against its unmodified base model (zero-shot Qwen2.5-3B). To establish true scientific credibility and measure the state-of-the-art, credible machine learning evaluation requires comparisons against other strong, similarly sized open-source medical models or traditional baselines.
•       Absence of Rigorous Error Analysis: The paper provides a confusion matrix, but lacks a deep error taxonomy. In healthcare, treating accuracy as the sole metric can be dangerous. The paper must provide a qualitative breakdown of its false positives versus false negatives, exploring why the model failed on 20% of its Top-1 predictions and analyzing the real-world clinical cost of those specific errors.
•       Out-of-Distribution (OOD) Testing: The current evaluation only tests 10 core case categories using automated metrics. To prove the model doesn't just memorize the synthetic data templates, it must be stress-tested on noisy, real-world, out-of-distribution clinical data.
•       Calibration and Clinical Safety: The model exhibits an Expected Calibration Error (ECE) of 0.275, meaning it is often overconfident, and its calibration is notably worst for Critical-severity conditions. Because miscalibrated probabilities are highly dangerous in medical triage, the authors suggest using the outputs as qualitative "relative rankings" instead of absolute percentages. The authors need to clarify exactly how this mitigation will be engineered into the user interface to prevent clinicians from being misled by overconfident, incorrect predictions.
•       Dataset Composition: While the paper details the training infrastructure and step count, the exact size, class balance, and generation methodology of the synthetic dataset need more explicit documentation. Proper data discipline and transparency are required to ensure the results are reproducible by other researchers.
•       Contradictory Loss Values (Text vs. Plot): The text caption for Fig. 2 explicitly states the per-epoch training losses as "Epoch 1: 0.3498, Epoch 2: 0.3224, Epoch 3: 0.5197". However, the actual embedded bar chart titled "Per-Epoch Loss" plots entirely different values: 0.6387, 0.5106, and 0.4107 respectively.
•       Systematic Figure Misnumbering: Every single figure referenced in the text has a caption number that does not match the hardcoded title inside the generated plot. For example, the text refers to "Fig. 3. Top-1 (80.0%) and Top-3 (100.0%)", but the image itself is titled "Fig 2 — Top-k Diagnostic Accuracy". The text references "Fig. 6. Top-1 diagnostic accuracy stratified by clinical severity", but the plot is titled "Fig 4 — Accuracy by Severity Level".
•       RAM/Memory Calculation Discrepancies: The abstract and Table IV state that the model achieves a peak inference RAM of "approximately 3 630 MB", which is a margin of 3,538 MB below the 7,168 MB ADTC ceiling. However, the introduction contradicts this, stating the peak RAM requirement is "approximately 3 730 MB — a margin of 3 438 MB below the ceiling".
•       Missing Table Data Provided in Charts: In Table III ("Main Evaluation Results"), the scores for ROUGE-2 (F1) and ROUGE-L (F1) are left completely blank with em-dashes ("—"). Yet, the exact values for these metrics (0.267 and 0.357) are visibly plotted in the accompanying "Fig 6 — Language Quality Metrics" chart.
•       Where are the other 40 Conditions?: The authors proudly state their dataset was curated to cover "50 clinical conditions" prevalent in East Africa. However, the entire clinical accuracy evaluation, including the confusion matrix, is only reported for 10 clinical case categories. The performance on the remaining 80% of the disease categories the model was trained on is entirely missing, leaving a massive gap in the evaluation.
•       "Perfect" Scores on Self-Generated Data: The authors boast a "perfect Top-3 score" (100.0%) and claim this represents genuine decision-support capability. However, because the evaluation dataset is generated from the exact same "hand-crafted clinical case templates" used to build the training set, this 100% score is an over-claim. It likely represents in-distribution dataset memorization rather than true clinical reasoning capability.
•       Which Quantization Yielded the Accuracy?: The paper highlights that it can run on edge devices using heavy compression formats like Q4_K_M and Q2_K (which drops the RAM to 2,990 MB). However, it never clarifies which quantization level was used to achieve the reported 80% Top-1 accuracy. If the 80% accuracy was achieved on the unquantized 16-bit model, but the hardware compliance was achieved on the Q2_K model, the hardware claims are disconnected from the clinical claims.
•       Conflict of Interest in Clinical Validation: In the limitations section, the authors attempt to justify their clinical deployability by stating an initial clinical evaluation is underway. However, they clarify this validation is being conducted by "P. B. Kasasira and C. B. Okoboi" — who are the co-authors of the paper. This introduces severe observer bias and requires explicit clarification on whether any blinding or independent third-party evaluation is being utilized to prevent the authors from grading their own model favorably.

This paper presents Aletheia, a clinical decision support tool that runs fully offline on an ordinary laptop, meant for health workers in places with poor internet and limited hardware. The authors fine-tune a 3B parameter model on clinical reasoning data weighted toward East African diseases, then shrink it so it fits on an 8 GB machine.

The engineering is the strongest part. Building something that works with no internet, on computers clinics already have, is the right answer for this setting, and the work is done carefully through to measuring how much memory it actually uses. The limitations section is also good. The authors are open about the training data being synthetic, the calibration being weak, and clinical testing not having happened yet.

The most important issue is that Section VI-D describes the results the wrong way round. The text says the model does best on test recommendation and evidence update, and worse on initial differential diagnosis. Figure 7 shows the opposite: test recommendation is the weakest at about 0.2, while initial differential diagnosis is among the strongest at about 0.8. This needs fixing.

My other concern is that the main results are hard to judge. The paper reports 80% Top-1 and 100% Top-3 accuracy across ten case categories, but never says how many actual cases sit inside those categories. Without that, a reader cannot tell whether the perfect Top-3 score comes from ten cases or several hundred, and those mean very different things. Table I also mentions 3,000 evaluation samples, but the paper does not explain how those relate to the reported results. One sentence giving the number of cases behind each figure, ideally with the raw counts, would clear this up. The authors do mention the narrow evaluation set in their limitations, but that note should sit next to the headline number rather than only at the end.

It is also worth noting that eight reasoning tasks are described in Section III-C but only five appear in the results. One of the missing three is red flag identification, which is about spotting when a patient needs urgent escalation. That is the most safety-critical part of the system, so it would be good to see it tested.

This is a useful and well-motivated piece of work, and the offline angle fills a real gap. The concerns are about how the results are presented rather than whether the work was done, and all can be fixed in a revision.

This paper presents Aletheia, a QLoRA-fine-tuned, GGUF-quantized 3B-parameter LLM (Qwen2.5-3B-Instruct base) for offline clinical decision support targeted at frontline health workers in Uganda/East Africa, explicitly engineered to meet the Africa Deep Tech Challenge 2026 hardware constraints (≤7,168 MB RAM, no GPU, no internet). This is a technically well-executed and practically motivated systems paper with a genuinely strong emphasis on real deployability rather than a benchmark.


The engineering pipeline is described in detailed and reproducible in principle; base model selection rationale, LoRA configuration (rank, target modules, hyperparameters all specified), a concrete two-stage quantization pipeline (F16 → Q4_K_M/Q2_K), and a formalized memory budget equation (Eq. 1) with a clear PASS against the ADTC ceiling with substantial margin.

The evaluation is reasonably multi-dimensional (Top-k accuracy, language-quality metrics including BERTScore/METEOR, calibration via ECE/Brier, and a base-vs-fine-tuned ablation).

The authors are also honest about limitations: they explicitly flagged the small evaluation set, moderate-to-poor calibration (ECE=0.275), the synthetic nature of most training data.

Here are a few things that authors should pay attention to;

1. First, the quantitative evaluation is conducted over only 10 core clinical case categories, and it's not clear from the text whether Top-1/Top-3 accuracy (80.0% / 100.0%) is computed over 10 individual cases or aggregated over a larger per-category sample, if it is the former, an "80.0%" accuracy is just 8/10 correct, and these headline numbers (used throughout the abstract and conclusion) carry far less statistical weight than their precision (one decimal place) implies. This should be clarified explicitly and, ideally, reported with confidence intervals or raw counts.

2. Secondly, there are several internal inconsistencies that suggest the paper needs a careful consistency pass before camera-ready: the article states the synthetic component has "20,000" samples and MedQA/MedMCQA contribute "9,358" and "10,000" respectively (Section III-A), but Table I reports 18,000/6,000/6,000 (60/20/20 split of 30,000) which brings a mismatch. Similarly, the abstract reports peak RAM of "~3,630 MB" while the introduction states "~3,730 MB" for the same quantity. Table III shows ROUGE-2 and ROUGE-L as "—" (missing), yet these same metrics are plotted with specific values (0.267, 0.357) in Fig. 8 which brings another unexplained gap between table and figure.

3. Thirdly, the training data is predominantly synthetic (60%) and hand-crafted by the authors themselves, with probability distributions for differentials described as reflecting "the authors' clinical knowledge synthesis rather than empirical epidemiological data"  Though this does mean the strong accuracy numbers should be read as evaluating fit-to-authors'-own-template rather than independent clinical ground truth, especially since MedQA/MedMCQA (the non-author-authored portion) make up a minority of both training and, presumably, evaluation data.

4. Lastly, no real clinical validation results are yet available; the "ongoing" clinician evaluation with co-authors Kasasira and Okoboi is mentioned only qualitatively ("early findings, which is encouraging") without any quantitative outcome reported in this paper.


The paper can be accepted, though there are a number of numerical inconsistencies highlighted above that should be corrected. In addition, the very small evaluation set should be much more prominently caveated wherever headline accuracy numbers are quoted (abstract, conclusion), rather than only in the limitations section.
